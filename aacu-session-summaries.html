Best evidence for the pitch

The strongest points for showing that other colleges are moving and that we are behind.

  • Community colleges are the furthest behind. A study of 253 colleges' public AI policies found 99 of 100 major research universities at "developing" or "maturing." Half of the associate's colleges (50 of 100) and 57 of 100 bachelor's colleges scored 0 or 1 on a 5-point scale, meaning almost nothing. (Brazelton, Northern Arizona University)
  • Most colleges allow AI. In the same study, 206 frameworks permit AI with conditions and only 26 prohibit it. Permissive frameworks also cover about twice as much ground (median 26.5 of 48 topics vs 12). (Brazelton)
  • It doesn't matter whether a policy was required from above or built locally. Both kinds came out about the same. "What matters is doing the work." (Brazelton)
  • Having no clear policy leaves students to sort it out. When the answer is "it depends," students taking five classes face five different rules and carry the risk of an integrity violation. One administrator said half of all academic dishonesty cases are now AI-related. (Melo, UNC Chapel Hill)
  • Students are afraid, not experts. Faculty in a 30-person community college AI Academy found many students "terrified" of being failed just for mentioning AI. (SOAR, Santa Monica College area) Students also asked for clearer acceptable-use rules and AI tutorials. (SUNY)
  • Faculty already expect change. In a survey of 1,000+ faculty, 95% expect students to rely on AI more, 90% worry it could weaken critical thinking, and 61% see potential to improve and personalize learning. (Opening plenary)
  • Teens already use AI companions. In a 2025 Common Sense Media survey, 72% of US teens had tried an AI companion and 52% used one at least a few times a month (n=1,060). (Bowen)
  • Design matters, not just access. In a high school math study, unrestricted AI helped during practice, but students did worse than a no-AI group once it was removed. A tutor with safeguards mostly avoided this. A carefully designed AI tutor in undergraduate physics beat active learning. (Cited by D'Amico. He didn't name them, but they match Bastani et al. (PNAS, 2025) and Kestin et al. (Harvard, Scientific Reports, 2025).)
  • Community colleges are already doing this. Harford CC (nursing tutors), LaGuardia CC (campus AI guide, first-year AI literacy modules, $50K in grants), Middlesex County College (AI in master syllabi), Santa Monica College (AI coaching, about 200 faculty at one drop-in), and Clark Atlanta (IBM AI literacy badge for every entering student).
  • Cost is not the barrier people assume. Harford's tutors run on BoodleBox's free tier. Alabama State, a lower-resource HBCU, built its own advising tools with staff who had no coding background. CUNY's grassroots "Don't AI Alone" group has no budget. On the other side, free tools ran out of capacity mid-project at UW Stevens Point, and one presenter pays about $120 a month out of pocket. That argues for institutional licenses.
  • Even skeptics' campuses invest. Georgetown's "Teaching Without AI" presenters work at a school with an enterprise Gemini license and a dedicated AI and Digital Learning Specialist position.

Classroom frameworks

Engineering Engagement: Socratic AI Tutors

Galen Ziegler, Assistant Professor of Nursing, Harford Community College (Maryland)

Community collegeNursingAI tutor
What they do
Builds course-specific AI tutors so nursing students get coaching at 2 AM, when he isn't available. The tutors replace rote rereading of notes. Each tutor is loaded with his own slides, objectives, study guides and exam blueprint, so it doesn't pull from the open internet.
Framework
Five pillars: (1) ask one question, then wait; (2) require reasoning and prioritization, not recall; (3) a safety alert that immediately corrects dangerous errors; (4) "knowledge bridges": a short explanation or analogy when stuck, then try again; (5) stay tied to the exam blueprint and redirect off-topic questions. Setup: pick one use case, attach materials, write instructions, test it as five kinds of students, revise.
Tool / cost
BoodleBox, free version. Includes a fill-in-the-blank system prompt template.
Results
Student perception survey: about 36% more students felt AI helps them understand complex concepts, 32% more felt it could improve performance, and 17% more felt comfortable using it. No sample size given, and these are perceptions, not grades. One student: "it would not just give me the answer, use hints and clues to lead me there."
Limits
Determined students can get around the instructions to obtain answers.
For the pitch: a community college, a free tool, and a low-lift model any department could copy.

TAKE AI.M: Preserving Engineering Judgment

Christina (last name not in transcript), Professor of Mechanical Engineering Technology, Ferris State University (Michigan)

Engineering / STEMCritical thinking
What they do
Students must commit to their own answer before consulting AI. Then an AI "board of experts" responds: one expert, one practitioner from a neighboring field, and one naysayer. Students judge each response.
Framework
Take a position (answer, confidence, reason) → AI assistance (the board) → Knowledge gained (did my answer change?) → Engineering judgment (correct? physically reasonable? assumptions valid? accept, partly accept, or reject each advisor) → AI.M reflection through one "M" lens (mistakes, misconceptions, method, modifications, merit, and others).
Tool
She built a web tool that forces the full cycle, because students kept skipping straight to AI. It exports a PDF log that students submit, and she grades the change in their thinking. On the instructor side, you can run your own assignment past the board to find AI shortcuts before students do.
Results
Example: a student designing a throwable water-rescue device changed his motor, battery and float decisions after the board pushed back. Not a formal study.
For the pitch: a STEM example that puts student judgment ahead of AI answers, with a graded paper trail.

From Answers to Judgment: the A.S.K.E.D. Framework

Scott D'Amico, Faculty Development Program Lead and adjunct political science instructor, Texas (college not named in the transcript)

Political scienceSimulationResearch cited
What they do
A semester-long simulation in which students act as political figures, for example a Texas governor deciding on the 2025 hemp/THC bill. Two AI advisors give confident, opposite advice. Then new information arrives (veterans oppose the ban), and students must decide again. A custom ChatGPT site walks students through it and produces a decision memo they submit in Canvas.
Framework
Articulate (what are we deciding?) · Support (what evidence?) · Knock around (strongest competing argument) · Evaluate (does it hold up after new information?) · Decide (recommendation, why, what is still uncertain). Repeat when new evidence arrives. No credit for changing your mind or for sticking with it, only for showing why.
Research cited
The two studies listed under "Design matters" in the evidence section above: unrestricted AI hurt later math performance, and a well-designed physics tutor beat active learning. Also research on reading comprehension and the self-monitoring that working with AI demands.
Other uses suggested
Case studies, bioethics, nursing diagnosis, IT, ethics bowl, group work in large lectures.
For the pitch: when answers are free, judging answers becomes the skill to teach. Includes two research citations.

Rewriting the Syllabus for the AI Era

Dr. Peri Yuksel, developmental psychologist, Kean University (New Jersey); a colleague in educational science and statistics, first name Nick (last name unclear); Dr. Nurdan Duzgoren-Aydin, academic leadership, Middlesex County College

Community college partnerPsychologySyllabus / policyResearch cited
Main idea
Set AI rules per assignment, not per course. Rules can even vary within one assignment (for example, AI for ideas, then for editing a draft you wrote). Scaffold AI use: the instructor demonstrates how they check AI output, and low-stakes practice comes before high-stakes work. If an assignment bans AI, say why and what skill students build instead.
Framework
Holistic Syllabus with a fifth dimension: AI pedagogy, fluency and responsible use. Uses the AI Assessment Scale levels: no AI, brainstorm, edit, AI generates and students critique, full use. A master blueprint maps AI literacy growth from 100- to 400-level courses using Bloom's taxonomy. "Inverted Bloom's": students jump straight to creating with AI and skip understanding and evaluating.
Leadership view
Course-by-course rules confuse students. Fix this at the program level with master course shells (especially for intro courses taught by adjuncts), curriculum maps that tie AI skills to program outcomes, employer expectations, and professional development in the strategic plan with a budget.
Examples
Statistics: devices away in class, plus a demo of AI misreading statistical output compared with APA standards. Comparing a database literature search with an AI search to show the gaps. Museum studies: fact-checking AI exhibit data. Computer science: pair programming with AI as "navigator."
Research cited
Perkins et al. 2024 (AI Assessment Scale); Ng et al. 2021 (AI literacy); Gurung & Galardi 2022 (syllabus tone affects help-seeking); Karve & Hurless 2026 ("Do I need an AI policy in the course syllabus?"); Anderson & Krathwohl 2001 (revised Bloom's); Yuksel & Bailey 2024. Free syllabus checklists on OSF.
For the pitch: a concrete, low-cost first step (department-level syllabus language), with a community college leader endorsing it and a strong citation list.

Infusing GenAI Across Disciplines (SUNY multi-campus project)

Audeliz Matias, SUNY Empire State University; Dr. Deepa Deshpande (lead researcher, Alfred State); team from SUNY Upstate, Fredonia and Schenectady; guest Rebecca Milliner, Associate Professor of Baking Production & Management, Alfred State College

State systemCross-disciplineMeasured study
Scale
SUNY grant, IRB-approved. Phase 2: 5 campuses, about 35 faculty (50 recruited), 639 students reached, 4 AI tools (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Perplexity through the vendor Mentor AI). Phase 1: 4 campuses, 27 faculty. Disciplines: STEM, health, culinary, business, social sciences, arts, humanities.
Framework
SIAC (Systematic Infusion of AI in Teaching): Design (start from a learning challenge in the discipline, choose the AI interaction, teach basic AI literacy first), Partner (student partners run a SWOT analysis on the faculty member's draft activity; paid $50), Measure (shared surveys on AI literacy, critical thinking and equity). Faculty had training, an online course, AI coaches and campus coordinators.
Results
Faculty AI literacy rose in both phases. Too few students completed the post-survey to measure them. Of 48 faculty-designed activities, 45 kept learning in the discipline central. AI was used for feedback (18), tutoring (14), critiquing AI output (13), content generation (13) and planning (11). Only 5 of 21 required students to verify information. Lesson: "Treat GenAI as a learning-design problem. Start with learning goals, not the tool."
Classroom example
Baking: seniors designed a bakery packaging line with two custom chatbots, one for creative ideas and one for restructuring the written report. Freshmen used AI for plated-dessert ideas. Some students turned in obvious AI filler, so she now writes an AI rubric for every project. Students who used it as intended "couldn't stop working on the project."
For the pitch: an entire state system running a coordinated, measured rollout across very different fields, including a hands-on culinary program.

Student AI Partners as Co-Designers

Haley Craig (learning experience designer) and Claudia Arcolin, University of Texas at San Antonio

Campus-wideStudent voice
What they do
A paid, structured 8 to 10 week internship, now in its 6th cohort (the first had 6 students). Students are matched with faculty, staff or student-service offices as co-designers, not assistants. They take Coursera AI literacy courses, join journal clubs, and do resume and communication training.
Framework
Pedagogical partnership (from Elon University's open guide): students and faculty learn AI together.
Outputs
A three-sentence syllabus AI statement that made a big difference for faculty and students. An "AI Essentials" guidelines course for faculty, staff and students, revised yearly. A student-led AI 101 workshop for staff. A student audit of 300+ chatbot FAQ answers. Assignment redesigns. Now moving into course transformation.
Results
Faculty said "I had no idea that's what students were thinking." Higher engagement. Lesson: start small, design for scale. (Johns Hopkins School of Education runs something similar with 5 students.)
For the pitch: a cheap way to start that gives students a voice and produces syllabus language and guides.

Creativity is a Choice: GenAI in Service Learning

Cary (last name not in transcript), media historian, Media Studies, University of Wisconsin-Stevens Point

Media studiesService learningSkeptic who teaches with AI
What they do
A 300-level course on AI and creativity (spring 2025). Students partnered with a local nonprofit to make K-5 bike trail safety videos and decided for themselves which AI tools, if any, to use at each production step. Each week paired a tool's history and ethics with a decision about using it. AI was never required.
Framework
"Creativity is a choice": every use of AI is a choice between outsourcing a piece of thinking and doing it yourself. Course arc: history of AI → theories of creativity → ethics → integration.
Assignments
Students wrote a vivid scene of a vacation 10 years from now, then asked Copilot to do the same. The AI answers were nearly identical (white-sand beach, half said the Maldives), while the human answers were all different. Another assignment had Copilot alter a creative piece students were proud of. It distorted their work and sometimes removed people of color, which opened a discussion of representation. Students also trained a simple dataset (Machine Learning for Kids).
Results
The client was pleased, but quality was below these students' usual work, partly because free tools ran out of capacity. The best video used AI only for the voice. Copilot was used because of the university's policy.
For the pitch: even a skeptic builds AI literacy into a course, and the lack of institutional tool access hurt the project.

From Pixels to Parameters: Design Engineering

Tamara (last name not in transcript), Graphic Design, University of Wisconsin-Stevens Point

Graphic designWorkforce
Main idea
AI is automating entry-level design production, so new graduates need senior-level skills from day one. A curriculum built only on manual software skills risks becoming obsolete.
Framework
"Design Engineering," with the graduate as director: teach design fundamentals first, then in 300/400-level courses teach design systems, design tokens, and connecting design tools (Figma, Adobe) to AI so students direct the AI rather than compete with it. Shift from a static portfolio to a "systems portfolio." Faculty teach themselves (Figma courses, "ask Claude to teach you Claude").
Example
"Personal Vision Quest": she started a career-planning app in Figma Make, and students rebuilt and extended it with job hotspots, salaries, BLS data and rent costs. Students ran out of AI credits and got frustrated, but learned to write prompts and iterate.
For the pitch: the workforce argument. Entry-level jobs are changing, and programs that don't adapt leave graduates behind.

From Players to Designers: Students Building AI-Assisted Sustainability Games

Patricia Kio, sustainability and the built environment (her college is not stated in the transcript), with guest lecturer Samuel Faladi of Texas A&M University

UniversitySustainabilityProject-based
What they do
About 32 students in two courses (economics of sustainability and life cycle assessment) design a web-based game that teaches a sustainability idea. One course was hybrid, the other in person. It was an individual assignment.
In the classroom
Students write a one-page game spec: learning target, player role, repeated action, feedback and score, and win condition. They give the spec to an AI to generate the game, then "negotiate" with it. They are told not to accept the first result. They ask the AI to write the prompt, ask for a game similar to a well-known one, then tell it what they don't like. The AI is "a resource to think with, not the source of answers." The AI returns HTML code, and students post it to GitHub so it runs as a web game. Classmates play 2 to 3 other games and give feedback in a Canvas discussion. Up to 18 games were made. The instructor polished two and tracked their GitHub traffic (up to about 50 views and 30 visitors in two weeks).
Tools
Students may pick any generative AI tool. The first round used Gemini and Copilot; this round uses Claude. GitHub (free account).
College support
None mentioned. All the tools are free. She said her college has no IRB approval for this, so she can't publish it as research yet.
Results
Sentiment analysis of peer comments: 83% positive, none negative, the rest neutral. Top themes: engagement, sustainability learning, systems thinking. Some students refuse to use AI. She teaches the content without AI first and lets them sketch a game by hand instead. The activity carries few points on purpose.
For the pitch: shows students building things with AI instead of only getting answers, with free tools and no special funding.

Institution-wide efforts

Beyond Compliance: AI Governance as a Sign of Digital Maturity

G. Blue Brazelton, Associate Professor of Higher Education Leadership and AI Fellow, Northern Arizona University

ResearchPolicyCommunity college data
Study
Coded the public AI policies of colleges randomly sampled from federal data: 100 each of research universities, master's, bachelor's and associate's colleges (253 had enough to code). A 600-college follow-up study is underway.
Findings
See the evidence section above: research universities 99 of 100 developing or maturing; associate's colleges 50 of 100 at nearly nothing; 206 permissive vs 26 prohibitive; required vs homegrown policies made no difference. Academic integrity was covered the same way everywhere. At some small colleges, one librarian's research guide was the whole AI framework. Cal State Fullerton's team-built framework was adopted by the entire Cal State system.
Framework
Digital maturity (Kane et al., The Technology Fallacy, 2019): early → developing → maturing. Mature organizations draw people toward change instead of ordering it. Six indicators: Default (no unless approved, or yes with conditions), Reach (beyond cheating: HR, tenure review, staff), Owner (someone's actual job), Current (dated, with a review schedule), Support (AI literacy training), Audiences (staff and student affairs, not just faculty). Free self-assessment web form.
Exemplar
Northeastern: chief AI officer, review board, and four assignment labels from "prohibited" to "required."
Also said
Students get rewarded for AI use in one class and charged with dishonesty for the same use in another. Colleges should license tools for students as an equity issue. An audience member who researches community colleges noted that two-year colleges' AI goals reflect their mission, for example helping ESL students.
For the pitch: the clearest data that associate's colleges are behind, plus a ready-made six-point checklist for where to start.

"It Depends": Ambiguity as AI Governance

Maggie Melo, Associate Professor, School of Data and Information Sciences, UNC Chapel Hill (NSF-funded)

ResearchPolicyStudents
Study
A scan of 16 UNC-system universities and interviews with 11 people who wrote AI guidance. Next phase: first-generation students' use of AI for academic help.
Findings
Colleges chose flexible guidance over formal policy on purpose, because AI changes too fast. Administrators are interpreting things day by day. Students were rarely involved. Quote: "two years ago there was no AI, and now half of all academic dishonesty things are somehow related to this new tool." No campus detection tool, and faculty want black-and-white answers.
Framework
Flexibility helps the institution and protects faculty autonomy, but at the student level it means five classes with five sets of rules, and the student carries the risk. Metaphor: a policy is a speed limit sign, but you also need roads, lane lines and pothole repair. Recommendations: concrete examples of acceptable use, support outside class (UNC's Library AI Studio, a shared space plus a chatbot), quick answers, and students on AI committees in paid roles. Students avoid asking for fear of being accused.
Research cited
A University of Hong Kong study (58 students, 12 teachers) on the hidden work students do to navigate unclear AI policies.
For the pitch: doing nothing is itself a policy, and its cost falls on students.

The Unexpected Upside: AI as a Catalyst for Educator Community

Mari (last name unclear), Hunter College / CUNY; J. Elizabeth Clark, AI Faculty Lead, LaGuardia Community College; Cassandra Silva Sibilin, York College and CUNY central office

Community collegeState systemFaculty development
Setting
CUNY: 26 colleges (7 community colleges), about 250,000 degree-seeking students.
Four models
  • Peer-led fellowship ("Building Bridges of Knowledge," 2024-25, private grant): 25 fellows in 5 discipline areas created open classroom materials teaching students to use AI. 200+ applicants from 21 colleges, 100% completion, and 13,000 downloads from 140 countries.
  • Institutional ecosystem (LaGuardia CC): "entered the ChatGPT era without a plan." In one year: sent a team to the AAC&U AI institute, held a campus AI conference (100+ faculty), wrote a student AI guide with a 6-level color scale (red 1 = no AI up to blue 5 = full use; purple 6 = AI is the topic), built 4 AI literacy modules for the first-year seminar, won two CUNY grants (about $50K), formed an advisory group, and ran a reading group on Bowen & Watson's Teaching with AI. The scale lets AI-averse faculty join the conversation too.
  • Collaborative network: the national AI in Education Network (3,000+ members) and its custom-chatbot interest group. Volunteer-run, unfunded.
  • Grassroots: "Don't AI Alone," started by about 10 CUNY colleagues. It runs conferences and a dashboard mapping who is doing what, with a session for skeptics coming up.
Lessons
Peer invitations work better than central-office emails. Go to department meetings instead of waiting for people to come to workshops.
For the pitch: a community college went from no plan to a campus-wide framework in one year on small grants. The grassroots model shows how to start with no budget.

Building an AI-Ready Faculty

SOAR grant project; scaled by Santa Monica College

Community collegeFaculty development
What they do
A faculty-led AI Academy: 30 faculty from career and technical ed, nursing, music, math and ESL. 27 completed projects, most shared as open resources. Projects included course redesigns, course management, data analysis and student chatbots.
Framework
Three anchors: purpose and readiness, conditions and support, sustainability. Not tied to any one tool. Choose-your-own-path assignments. AI coaches (trained in coaching, not tech) check in weekly, plus monthly drop-in "Coaching Corners."
Results
Santa Monica College funded ongoing coaching for all faculty. One drop-in drew about 200 people. Faculty found many students "terrified" of AI. Barriers: burnout, time, union issues, policy, and worry that tools will disappear. Course materials and forms are free to reuse.
For the pitch: a community college faculty development model with a ready-made toolkit.

Optimizing and Modernizing Student Success through AI

Dr. Rena Bristol (retired Assistant Provost and Graduate Dean) and Ayo Atane (Academic Program Coordinator), Alabama State University, an HBCU in Montgomery

HBCUAdvising / student successLow cost
What they do
Faculty and staff with no coding background used the university's closed internal AI system ("AskStinger") to build tools. They discovered faculty were already doing this on their own and brought them together. Tools: a student degree tracker dashboard (progress, alerts, links), degree audit logic written by faculty, and a transfer credit analyzer for rising transfer and dual-enrollment volume. Governance
IT, institutional research and the VP were involved. One project was paused over data security. Open questions: who maintains a faculty-built tool, and is it accessible?
For the pitch: a resource-limited college built its own AI tools in-house, and AI can serve advising and retention, not just classrooms.

From Experimentation to Framework: Teaching with AI

Leandro de Castro, Professor and Director of the AI and Data Science Institute, Florida Gulf Coast University (Whitaker College of Engineering)

Public universityFaculty fellowshipFramework + free prompt library
What they do
In 2025 FGCU began an "AI University" initiative with two parts: AI in student success and teaching, and AI in operations. Its first step was a Faculty AI Fellowship: 10 fellows from arts and sciences, health, business, entrepreneurship and engineering each brought AI into a course for a full academic year and documented what happened. Courses covered 7 disciplines, including health care delivery, global business, statistics, AI-based startups, biofluid mechanics and environmental data science. Techniques included custom GPTs.
Framework
Pedagogical Integration, seven pillars: (1) intentional role definition, say exactly what AI is for; (2) progressive integration, no AI first, then more as skills grow (like teaching math before the calculator); (3) prompt literacy; (4) question the output; (5) verify first; (6) hybrid assessment, including paper and oral work; (7) responsible use. Instrumental Augmentation, seven steps for the instructor: planning, class design, assignments, class delivery, student support, assessment and feedback, and course improvement.
In the classroom
Example: in a data analysis course students got a synthetic health data set. They could describe the columns to the AI but could not paste in data. The AI wrote the Excel formulas, and students ran them in Excel or Google Sheets. He now uses paper and oral assessments because online work is no longer a reliable measure. He says students used to dislike AI in class but now see it as more than an assignment maker.
Tools
ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity (all offer "projects" or workspaces). Two tools he recommends: an AI workspace for the instructor (a project that holds course files and the history of your work with them) and a personalized AI assistant for students (a tutor where the instructor controls what students can ask). Students also use an open prompt library.
College support
A small stipend for each fellow, a 100-point selection rubric, and a new 3-year research grant to run the framework university-wide. A new cohort of fellows starts next semester. FGCU launched a fully online "Teaching with AI" course in its AI Virtual Academy this week. The prompt library and mind map are free and open to anyone. The state system also uses a shared syllabus tool called Simple Syllabus.
Results
Findings are from fellows' teaching notes, not a controlled study. Next semester they plan to measure knowledge gained on specific topics.
For the pitch: a model for how a college funds a small group to test AI for a year and then scales it. The prompt library is free to borrow now.

From Textbooks to Chatbots: Course-Specific AI

Laura Landon, Assistant Professor of Occupational Therapy, Maryville University (St. Louis, Missouri)

Private universityHealth / graduateCourse chatbot
What they do
A chatbot built for one graduate anatomy course. Students weren't reading the textbook and were getting conflicting answers from different search engines and AI summaries. The chatbot answers only from her vetted material.
How it works
Trained on her assignments, lectures and slides (including instructor versions with her notes), transcripts of every recorded lecture, an open access textbook and her assignment answer keys. It uses the keys to guide answers but never reveals them. It sits inside Canvas as a chat bubble on the course page. Every answer links to the slide or PDF it came from. Images come from her own slides, not AI generated, because she was uneasy about AI-generated anatomy images.
Tools
A custom chatbot built by the university's internal AI team, embedded in Canvas. No outside product was named.
College support
The university's internal AI team built it, and it took about a year. No dollar amounts given.
Results
Before building: 79% of students said they'd use one and 87% said it would improve their learning. Top requests were practice questions like her exams, images and reliable answers. Once launched, 100% of students used it all semester. They called it useful, easy and trustworthy, and wanted one in every course. Mixed-method pilot, one course.
Limits and next step
Students now lean on the chatbot more than the textbook, so she worries about over-reliance and less critical thinking. She is building a Socratic version that asks questions first, and wants to see whether students will use a chatbot that doesn't give quick answers.
For the pitch: the closest match to a course-specific tool in your own LMS. It shows students will use it, and it also shows the over-reliance risk.

Day 2 Plenary: Human Thinking and AI

José Antonio Bowen (co-author of Teaching with AI)

Teaching and learningResearch cited
Main ideas
Results depend on how teaching is designed, not on access to AI. Passively reading AI explanations is like highlighting: it feels like learning but isn't. Good AI tutors ask more than they tell. Assignments should make changes in thinking visible: commit, defend, get feedback, revise, explain what changed. Sort skills into: done unaided, safe to delegate, and done with AI. Colleges need to be a "cognitive gym" that keeps the useful mental effort.
Examples
AI-built simulations: Three Mile Island control room, Broad Street cholera outbreak, a policy-modeling assignment, and a database of 250,000 piano works.
Research cited
Common Sense Media 2025: 72% of teens have tried an AI companion. Gallup-Purdue 2014: only 27% of graduates strongly agreed their professors cared about them as people. Harvard Business School's $699 program with AI professor avatars. "Verification is the new limit" (slide cites Cunningham 2026 and Brodeur et al. 2026, PNAS): AI lets you produce more than you can check.
Q&A
Admits long-term evidence is limited. Opposes a policing approach. Says to tell students each assignment's purpose.
For the pitch: the "design, not access" message, plus the teen companion statistic and the verification slide.

Faculty workload and counterpoint

Reclaim Your Time, Reclaim Your Agency

G. Blue Brazelton, Northern Arizona University

Faculty productivity
What they do
Uses AI for faculty work, not student work: drafting, editing, research triage, slide decks, assessment reports. It never touches student data or assignments.
Framework
MAPS (multi-agent persona system): a set of specialized AI "personas" (editor, researcher and fact-checker, storyteller, project manager), each just a file of instructions. A second AI reviews the work, and the human stays in charge. Start with a "Monday action": name the project you keep putting off, find the tedious step, and hand that step to AI first.
Equity
Paid tools and uneven access are a barrier. He pays about $120 a month himself and urges colleges to provide enterprise seats or reimbursement. He calls the gains "narrow, real gains, not magic."
Classroom ideas from the chat
A University of Arizona nutrition professor lets students improve their writing with AI but requires them to verify and cite every scientific change. A graphic design professor replaced online discussion boards ("the single most AI-able assignment") with conversational bots. Her three questions before using AI: Do I know enough to judge the output? Would I otherwise pay a person for this? Would this exist without AI? Brazelton offers an opt-out track for students who object.
For the pitch: AI can give faculty time back for teaching, and the college should pay for the tools, not individual faculty.

Teaching Without AI

David Ebenbach (Assistant Director, Graduate Student & Faculty Programs) and Ella Csarnó (AI and Digital Learning Specialist), Georgetown University teaching center

CounterpointResearch cited
Stance
Ebenbach is openly "AI hostile." Csarnó supports faculty in using AI but is conflicted about it. They model respectful disagreement. Both agree that teaching without AI can't mean teaching the way we used to: faculty need to know what AI can do and how students use it, including as companions and therapists.
Framework
Meet the needs students turn to AI for, without AI. Personalization: choice, reflection, relationships, office hours, peer review. Non-judgmental help: say out loud that you are a resource. Availability: peer review, online resources. Intercultural support. Also reduce the temptation to misuse it: explain your reasons, build community, use real-world problems, give frequent low-stakes assignments (high stakes push students to AI), require drafts, and reward risk-taking. Teach AI literacy even in no-AI courses.
Research cited
AI feedback on essays changed with randomly assigned ethnicity and gender (Latino writers were told to work on English; women were told to be more likable). Georgetown research: college essays since 2022 have richer vocabulary but fewer distinct ideas. Dewsbury's intro biology study: covering 25% less content to build community gave equal final exam scores and slightly better results in the next course.
For the pitch: use this to answer skeptical colleagues. Even here, the argument is for AI literacy, and Georgetown still has an enterprise Gemini license and a staff position for AI.
